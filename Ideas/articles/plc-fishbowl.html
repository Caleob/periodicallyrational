PLC Fishbowl: Closing the Teacher Reflection Gap

Can a synthetic advisory panel cure teacher amnesia and make instructional reflection psychologically safe?

By Caleob King • Published October 2026 • 7 min read

🐟 Interactive Tool: PLC Fishbowl

Teachers get better through reflection. But few of us can afford the time to watch our colleagues teach, and fewer still are brave enough to ask peers into our rooms often enough to tighten that feedback loop. Can a multi-agent AI panel close that gap?

The Observation Dilemma - Why Feedback Can Be Terrifying

In public education, evaluation is a dirty word that carries the weight of a whole administrative leviathan. Even in healthy school cultures, the mechanics of observation carry deep emotional baggage. Asking a trusted colleague down the hall to sit in on your 3rd period Algebra 2 class isn't just logistically fraught - stealing their precious planning period to watch you work - it is an uncomfortable cocktail of embarrassment and anxiety.

What if the lesson falls flat? What if I am imposing on their friendship? What if I simply don't measure up to their standards?

We know instinctively that comparison is the thief of joy. Yet we also know that rigorous, honest reflection is our single best vehicle toward instructional mastery. Caught between the fear of scrutiny and the desire to improve, most teachers default to solitude. Formal evaluations happen once or twice a year, dominated by compliance rubrics, state checklists, and delayed administrative feedback. Daily instructional reflection shrinks to an exhausted sigh during your 5-minute passing period while you scramble to unjam the photocopier.

To break this deadlock, I wanted an environment that was radically low-stakes, completely private, and available on demand. What if teachers had access to an advisory board designed purely for formative growth - with zero evaluative teeth?

Remixing Silicon Valley for the Classroom

The inspiration for this experiment came from outside education. Media producer and technologist Gavin Purcell built a multi-agent framework that simulated a startup advisory panel. An entrepreneur could pitch a raw business idea, and a roundtable of specialized venture capitalists, operators, and growth hackers would debate the merits in a live virtual fishbowl while the founder watched from the sidelines.

Why shouldn't classroom teachers have the same strategic infrastructure?

I remixed Purcell's architecture into PLC Fishbowl, transforming the startup boardroom into a digital Professional Learning Community. But before running the experiment, I had to solve the biggest hurdle in educational AI: student privacy.

I did not upload student work, video feeds, or class rosters. Instead, I recorded and generated an audio transcript of ONLY my side of the lesson. Every student question, response, and identity was scrubbed by design - preserving zero student Personally Identifiable Information. What remained was a raw, unfiltered transcript of my actual instructional craft: my verbal cues, wait times, framing of mathematical concepts, pacing, analogies, and classroom management transitions.

The Fishbowl processes this transcript across a structured 3-round dialogue:

First, initial takes where each synthetic panelist reviews the transcript through their specific pedagogical lens. Second, cross-talk and debate where the panelists interrogate one another, challenging competing pedagogical priorities. Third, teacher interjection and wrap-up where the educator can pose targeted follow-ups before each advisor issues a final, high-leverage takeaway.

Meet the Council - Four Lenses on One Lesson

If you ask a generic AI chatbot to evaluate your lesson, you get a generic list of bulleted platitudes. The power of a fishbowl lies in distinct, competing pedagogical tensions. For my lesson introducing complex numbers and the complex plane in Algebra 2, I assembled a council of 4 distinct archetypes:

🚌 Mrs. Frizzle

The STEAM & Manipulatives Champion - passionate about embodied cognition, tactile tools, and getting students' virtual hands dirty with interactive models instead of passive viewing.

🖖 Mr. Spock

The Instructional Architect - laser-focused on dual coding theory, schema activation, working memory saturation, and strict structural boundaries.

📋 Mrs. Knope

The Relational Anchor - focused on affective safety, belonging, mitigating student imposter syndrome, and validating the emotional struggle of learning hard math.

🧙‍♂️ Gandalf

The Humanist Philosopher - looking past mechanical formulas to the deeper grammar of reality, language, and conceptual reverence.

The "Display Your Standards" guy

I don't like him. I may need him

We all know the instructional architect type in real life. In many schools, that is the district compliance coach who walks into your room with a clipboard, ignores the warm rapport you've built with 25 restless teenagers, and marks you down because the objective wasn't written on the top-right corner of the whiteboard in dry-erase marker. In person, that interaction produces defensive dread.

Yet inside the Fishbowl, the dynamic shifts completely. I don't love the rigid compliance guy in the flesh, but pedagogically, I might actually need him - provided I have total psychological agency. With an AI advisor, I can invite the rigid persona into my room, listen to his unsparing audit of my lesson pacing, take the medicine that is useful, and close the browser tab with complete impunity. There is no evaluation matrix, no union grievance, and no permanent record in my HR personnel file.

The Sycophancy Problem - And the Spoonful of Sugar Defense

Let's be completely honest: the tool is not without flaws. Like most large language model applications, the initial feedback suffers from reflexive sycophancy. The opening round is packed with praise. Mrs. Frizzle announced that her "tiger-avoiding brain does a happy dance," while Gandalf declared that I had "masterfully invited students across the digital threshold."

If you're looking for ruthless, unvarnished peer critique, this cheerleading can feel cloying. But in practice, I discovered something counterintuitive - the praise acts as a spoonful of sugar. Because teaching is personal and emotionally taxing, the initial affirmation lowers your defensive walls. You don't brace for impact; your affective filter drops.

And once those defenses are lowered, the panel's actual critiques land with surprising sharpness.

Mr. Spock on Instructional Architecture:
"Mr King dedicated a significant portion of instructional time to administrative announcements regarding course corrections and grading policies during the synchronous window. This administrative intrusion risks saturating working memory right after a heavy cognitive lift involving the complex plane. My structural recommendation is to redirect asynchronous housekeeping tasks entirely to the learning management system message board... Instructional efficacy is maximized when the synchronous virtual environment is strictly reserved for guided cognitive architecture."

Spock caught a real bad habit that I had rationalized for months. Right after demanding intense cognitive work from students - visualizing orthogonal axes on a complex plane - I squandered the final 5 minutes of class rattling off due dates and assignment policies. Spock rightly diagnosed this as cognitive overload: contaminating working memory right when an exit ticket or reflection loop should have cemented the new schema.

Then Gandalf chimed in with a pedagogical critique that stopped me in my tracks:

Gandalf on Mathematical Linguistics:
"When we call a number imaginary, we whisper to the student that it dwells in a falsehood, in some phantom shadowland disconnected from truth. Mathematics is not an inventory of tricks. It is the grammar of creation itself. My single most important actionable insight for your online classroom is to banish the word imaginary from your vocabulary for one entire lesson. Replace it with lateral or vertical or orthogonal, inviting students to see these numbers not as ghosts haunting the equations, but as travelers walking upon a wider, richer road."

That wasn't generic AI summary; that was profound pedagogical insight. In fact, Gandalf was channeling an argument made in 1831 by Carl Friedrich Gauss, who lamented that if $+1$, $-1$, and $\sqrt{-1}$ had been named direct, inverse, and lateral units rather than positive, negative, and imaginary, generations of students wouldn't have been baffled by their legitimacy. A math teacher can teach for 20 years without ever examining how a single historical misnomer alienates young learners.

Curing Teacher Amnesia - The True Artifact

Let's be unmistakably clear - this is not a replacement for watching human colleagues teach, nor does it replace having a trusted peer observe your classroom. The irreplaceable empathy of a real teacher sitting in the back of your room - noticing the quiet kid in the second row who finally raised his hand - cannot be replicated by a silicon prompt.

What the Fishbowl does solve is education's silent productivity killer - teacher amnesia.

Every educator knows this cycle. You teach 180 days a year across multiple preps. In October, you deliver a lesson on complex numbers. You realize halfway through that your vector sketches were static, that the word imaginary confused third period, and that your administrative announcements killed your exit ticket. You vow to fix it.

By next October, those realizations have vanished into the ether. You pull up the same slide deck, teach the same lesson, and make the exact same micro-mistakes.

The Reflective Artifact

By saving the PLC Fishbowl output directly into my unit folder alongside the lesson plan, I have preserved a living instructional debrief. Next fall, when I open my Complex Numbers unit, I won't just see my slides; I will see Spock's reminder to push administrative bulletins to the LMS, Frizzle's nudge to provide interactive GeoGebra sliders, and Gandalf's challenge to introduce them as lateral coordinates.

Teacher reflection shouldn't require risking your ego, monopolizing a colleague's planning block, or waiting for a high-stakes administrator walkthrough. By treating synthetic AI personas not as evaluators, but as an on-demand sounding board, we can build the reflective stamina we need - one lesson at a time.
